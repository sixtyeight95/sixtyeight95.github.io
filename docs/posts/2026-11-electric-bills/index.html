Why Does My Electric Bill Keep Going Up?

Electricity prices are up about 40% since 2020. AI data centers are part of the story, but not all of it.

If your electric bill seems higher every time you open it, you’re not imagining it. We looked at government price data to see how much electricity has really gone up, how that compares with paychecks, and what’s driving it.

The short answer

The average price of electricity is 19.0¢ per kWh, up 42% since January 2020, and up 5.4% in the last year alone. For a typical home, that’s a monthly bill of about $171, versus $120 in early 2020.

The drivers: power-hungry AI data centers, expensive grid upgrades, and natural gas prices, with experts still arguing about how much each one matters.

The typical monthly bill

We used the Energy Information Administration’s figure for average home use, about 899 kWh a month, and the government’s average price per kWh. For most of the 2010s, the typical bill barely moved. Since 2021, it has climbed steadily.

In hours of work

As in our other posts, we also measured the bill in hours of work for the average worker:

This puts the jump in perspective. The monthly bill takes about 5.4 hours of work today, up from 4.9 in 2021. That’s a real increase, but it’s still well below the 1980s, when a similar bill took 7 to 8 hours. Electricity got much cheaper relative to pay for decades; the last few years reversed some of that.

What’s driving it

1. Data centers. The AI boom runs on enormous data centers, and they use a lot of power. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report for the Department of Energy found data centers used 4.4% of all U.S. electricity in 2023, and projected 6.7% to 12% by 2028. More demand can push up prices, especially in regions where data centers are clustered, and utilities are building new capacity to serve them.

2. The grid itself. Utilities are spending heavily to replace aging equipment, build new power lines, and harden the grid against storms and wildfires. Those costs are passed on to customers, and they rise even when the cost of generating power doesn’t.

3. Natural gas. Natural gas is the biggest fuel for U.S. electricity, so its price swings flow through to power prices. The energy shock of 2022 and this year’s oil and gas disruptions both played a role.

How much is each one? Honestly, nobody has a clean answer. Government data counts data centers inside “commercial” electricity use, so their exact effect on household bills is hard to isolate, and researchers disagree on the split. Where you live matters a lot: bills have jumped most in regions with tight power supplies and fast data center growth.

Ways to fight back

  • Check for a cheaper plan or time-of-use rate. Some utilities charge much less at night.
  • Look for state and utility rebates on efficient appliances, heat pumps, and insulation.
  • Ask about budget billing to smooth out big seasonal swings.

The fine print

  • National averages. Electricity prices vary enormously by state, from well under to well over double the national average.
  • The “typical bill” uses average usage and the average price per kWh; real bills include fixed monthly charges and taxes.
  • Pay is average hourly earnings before taxes.

The bottom line

Electricity really has gotten more expensive, fast: about 40% since 2020. Data centers are a real and growing part of the story, alongside grid spending and fuel costs. In work time it’s not as bad as the 1980s, but the long trend of electricity getting cheaper has clearly reversed.
