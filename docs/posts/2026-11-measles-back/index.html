Measles Is Back. Here’s What the Data Shows.

The U.S. just lost its ‘measles-free’ status after 26 years. Cases are the highest in more than three decades, and kindergarten vaccination rates keep slipping.

On November 17, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) announced that the United States has lost its measles elimination status, a designation it had held since 2000. Here’s what that means, how we got here, and what the numbers say.

The short answer

The U.S. has recorded 5,412 measles cases so far in 2026, the most in more than 30 years and more than double last year’s total. 96% of this year’s cases were in people who were unvaccinated or didn’t know their status. Meanwhile, the share of kindergartners with both measles shots has fallen from 95.2% to 92.4% since 2019-20, below the roughly 95% that experts say is needed to stop outbreaks.

What “losing elimination status” means

“Eliminated” never meant zero cases. It meant measles wasn’t spreading continuously inside the country. Cases came in from travelers, caused small outbreaks, and then died out.

Losing the status means a single chain of measles spread here nonstop for more than 12 months. PAHO’s commission found that had happened in the U.S., as well as in Mexico and Bolivia. Canada lost its status in 2025.

To get it back, the U.S. has to go 12 straight months without that kind of continuous spread.

The cases

From 2000 to 2018, the U.S. averaged about 141 measles cases a year. Then came 2019 (1,274 cases), 2025 (2,242), and now 2026, at 5,412 and counting.

This year so far, according to the CDC:

  • 590+ people have been hospitalized.
  • At least 6 people have died, and some states have reported additional deaths.
  • Cases have been confirmed in 45 states plus Washington, D.C.
  • The largest outbreak is in Pennsylvania, with more than 1,500 cases.

Why it’s happening: fewer kids are vaccinated

Measles is one of the most contagious diseases there is. To keep it from spreading, experts say about 95% of people need to be immune. Kindergarten vaccination rates have been below that line for six straight years.

In the 2025-26 school year:

  • Only 10 states reached the 95% mark. Coverage ranged from West Virginia (98.9%) to Idaho (75.2%).
  • About 280,000 kindergartners started school without records showing both MMR doses.
  • Exemptions hit a record 4.2%. Almost all of them were non-medical.

National averages also hide local pockets. A state at 93% can have schools and communities far below that, and that’s where outbreaks take off.

What it means for you

  • The vaccine works. Two doses of MMR are about 97% effective. Only 2% of this year’s cases were in fully vaccinated people.
  • Not sure if you’re covered? Ask your doctor. A blood test can check, or you can simply get another dose; there’s no harm in an extra one.
  • Some people can’t be vaccinated, including babies under 12 months, some people with weakened immune systems, and pregnant women. They depend on everyone around them being immune.

The fine print

  • Case counts are confirmed cases reported to the CDC. The 2026 figure is as reported on November 17 and will rise. The true number of infections is likely higher.
  • Death counts differ between the CDC and some states while reports are confirmed.
  • Kindergarten figures are national estimates from state school-entry reports.
