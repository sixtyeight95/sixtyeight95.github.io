Is Inflation Back?

Prices are rising faster again, mostly because of gas. And for the first time in years, paychecks are falling behind.

After the 2021–2023 price surge finally cooled, a lot of people hoped inflation was behind us. This year, it started climbing again. We looked at the government’s own numbers to see how big the rebound is, what’s driving it, and what it means for your paycheck.

The short answer

Prices are up 4.1% from a year ago (October 2026), up from 2.7% in February 2026. Most of the jump is energy: gasoline is up 37.4%. Without food and energy, inflation is a calmer 2.9%.

But it’s enough to bite: pay is up only 3.3%, so the average paycheck now buys less than it did a year ago for the first time since early 2023.

The rebound

The dashed line is the Federal Reserve’s 2% target. The dotted line marks the U.S. and Israeli strike on Iran in late February 2026, which disrupted oil shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Notice that the red line (all prices) jumped right after, while the teal line (everything except food and energy) barely moved. That tells you this rebound is mostly about energy, not a broad-based surge like 2022.

What’s driving it

Two things to watch:

  • Wholesale prices are up 6.5%. Producer prices measure what businesses pay. When those rise faster than consumer prices, some of it tends to show up on store shelves later.
  • Food is starting to climb. Food prices rose 2.9% over the year, and higher fuel and shipping costs can push grocery prices up with a delay.

What it means for your paycheck

This line shows whether paychecks are beating prices. Above zero, the average worker’s pay is growing faster than prices; below zero, it’s falling behind. From mid-2023 through 2025, pay comfortably outran prices. In October 2026, it dipped below zero: -0.3%.

The Fed is raising rates again

To fight inflation, the Federal Reserve raises interest rates, which makes borrowing more expensive and cools spending. After holding rates steady for most of 2026, the Fed has started nudging them up: the federal funds rate rose from 3.63% to 3.88% this fall, and many forecasters expect another increase in December. For households, that means higher rates on credit cards, car loans, and mortgages.

The fine print

  • Inflation numbers are averages. If you drive a lot, energy inflation hits you much harder than someone who works from home.
  • “Core” inflation leaves out food and energy because they swing wildly; it’s a better guide to the long-term trend, but you still pay for gas and groceries.
  • Pay is the average hourly earnings of production and nonsupervisory workers, before taxes.
  • This can change quickly. If oil prices fall (as they did briefly this month on hopes of calmer fighting with Iran), headline inflation could ease fast.

The bottom line

Inflation is back, but it’s a different animal than 2022: so far it’s mostly an energy shock, with everything else rising at a more normal pace. That’s the good news. The bad news is that it’s enough to put paychecks behind prices again, and the Fed’s response means borrowing is getting more expensive too.
