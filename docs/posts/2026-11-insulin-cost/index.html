Insulin Costs About $5 a Vial to Make. Here’s What Countries Actually Pay.

The cheap-to-make claim is true. The famous U.S. sticker price was real for some people, but it was never the real price.

You’ve probably seen the claim: insulin costs a few dollars to make, but Americans pay hundreds. Insulin was discovered more than 100 years ago, and its inventors famously sold the patent to the University of Toronto for $1 each so it would stay affordable. So how did a vial end up costing more than a car payment?

We dug into the research to see what insulin really costs to make, what other wealthy countries pay, and what the U.S. number actually means.

The short answer

A vial of insulin (1,000 units) costs an estimated $3 to $9 to make, including a profit margin. In 2022, other wealthy countries paid an average of $24 per vial. The U.S. list price averaged $227.

But that list price is not what insurers actually paid. After secret discounts, the U.S. paid about $54, still about 2.3 times other countries. And a lot has changed since 2022.

What it costs to make

In 2018, researchers published a detailed estimate in BMJ Global Health of what insulin would cost if many companies competed to make it, the way they do for generic pills. They added up the raw ingredients, filling and packaging the vials, building factories, getting regulatory approval, shipping, and a 20% margin for overhead and profit.

One 10 mL vial = 1,000 units. Source: Gotham, Barber & Hill, BMJ Global Health (2018).
Type of insulin Estimated price per vial
Regular human insulin $3.29 – $4.86
NPH (human) $3.35 – $4.93
Glargine (Lantus-type) $5.32 – $7.38
Lispro (Humalog-type) $6.51 – $8.87
Aspart (Novolog-type) $6.50 – $8.86

So yes, the viral claim holds up: insulin is cheap to make. Even the newer, longer-lasting insulins come in under $10 a vial.

What these estimates don’t include: the original research that created each insulin, the pharmacy’s markup, or insurance. They’re an estimate of what a competitive market could charge, not what any company actually spends.

What wealthy countries pay

The RAND Corporation compared insulin prices in the U.S. and 33 other wealthy countries using 2022 sales data. These are the prices drugmakers charge before discounts, averaged across all the insulin each country uses.

The U.S. isn’t just a little higher. At $227 per vial, it was about 9.7 times the average of the other 33 countries (RAND’s careful like-for-like comparison). Canada, often the go-to comparison, was $38.

Notice something else, though: every country pays well above the cost to make it. The UK pays roughly 2 to 7 times the estimated production cost. Everyone pays a markup. The U.S. markup was just in a different league.

Why the U.S. sticker price was never the real price

Here’s the part that rarely makes the viral posts. In the U.S., drugmakers set a high list price, then pay big secret rebates to the companies that manage insurance drug plans (called pharmacy benefit managers, or PBMs) in exchange for being covered. RAND estimates those discounts knocked about 76% off the U.S. list price.

So who paid the $227? Often the people with the least bargaining power: the uninsured, and people in high-deductible plans, whose out-of-pocket costs are frequently based on the list price. That’s how insulin became a symbol of American health care, even though insurers were paying far less.

Researchers still argue over who’s to blame for the system. Some point to drugmakers raising list prices; others point to middlemen who earn more when list prices and rebates are both high. Either way, even after the discounts, the U.S. still paid about 2.3 times what other wealthy countries paid.

What’s changed since 2022

The RAND data is from 2022, and the U.S. insulin market has changed a lot since then:

  • List price cuts. In 2023, Eli Lilly cut list prices for its main insulins by about 70%. Novo Nordisk’s cuts of 65–75% and Sanofi’s 78% cut to Lantus took effect in January 2024.
  • $35 caps. Since 2023, people on Medicare pay no more than $35 a month for insulin. Some states, including California, cap it for many private plans too, and the big makers offer $35 programs.
  • New low-cost insulin. In January 2026, California’s CalRx program and the nonprofit drugmaker Civica launched a long-acting insulin at no more than $55 for five pens, about $11 a pen. That works out to about $37 per 1,000 units, right in line with what Canada paid in 2022.

These changes don’t show up in the 2022 numbers, so today’s U.S. list prices are lower than our chart shows.

The fine print

  • Different years, different sources. The cost estimate is from 2018; the country prices are from 2022.
  • Country prices are averages across all the insulin each country buys, so a country that uses more of the newer insulins will look a bit pricier.
  • U.S. discounts are estimated. Rebates are confidential, so RAND applied an average discount.
  • No country’s price includes the pharmacy’s markup or taxes. What patients pay at the counter can differ.

The bottom line

Insulin really is cheap to make, roughly $3 to $9 a vial. Other wealthy countries pay a few times that. The U.S. list price was about ten times higher than theirs, but it mostly functioned as a starting point for secret discounts, and the people who paid it were the ones least able to. After a wave of price cuts, caps, and new low-cost options since 2023, the gap is closing. But it hasn’t closed.
