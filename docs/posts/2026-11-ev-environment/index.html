Are Electric Cars Really Better for the Environment?

On carbon, yes, after about two years of driving. But ‘zero emissions’ hides real damage that happens somewhere else.

In our last post, we found that a new EV usually isn’t cheaper to own than a gas car. So the other big argument for EVs is the environment. But you’ve probably heard the counterargument: making the batteries is so dirty that EVs aren’t really greener. Is that true?

We built a simple, transparent model from government and research data you can check, and compared it with the major lifecycle studies.

The short answer

Building an EV’s battery creates about 4.5 tons of extra CO2 before it drives a single mile. But an EV emits so much less per mile that it pays that back after about 24,000 miles, under two years of typical driving. Over its life, a U.S. EV produces roughly half to two-thirds less CO2 than a comparable gas car.

The catch: the mining behind those batteries causes real harm, mostly far from where EVs are driven.

The crossover

Here’s the whole story in one chart. The EV starts behind because of its battery. Then, every mile, the gas car burns gasoline while the EV draws power from the grid, which on average emits much less.

The dashed line is the break-even point: about 24,000 miles, or about 1.6 years at 15,000 miles a year. After that, every mile the EV drives puts it further ahead.

We kept this deliberately simple and, if anything, tilted toward the gas car: we count only gasoline burned in the engine, not the emissions from drilling, refining, and trucking it to the station. We also left out the rest of the car (frame, seats, glass), which takes roughly the same emissions to build either way.

How that compares with the big studies

  • The International Council on Clean Transportation (2021) found that a medium-size EV bought in the U.S. produces 60% to 68% less greenhouse gas over its lifetime than a comparable gas car.
  • The Union of Concerned Scientists (2022) found EVs produce about half the lifetime emissions, with an average break-even around 21,000 miles.
  • BloombergNEF estimated a typical U.S. EV breaks even after about 25,000 miles, roughly two years.

Our simple model lands right in that range: about 54% less CO2 over 150,000 miles.

It depends on where you plug in

An EV is only as clean as the power plant charging it. On the average U.S. grid, an EV emits about 107 grams of CO2 per mile. Charged entirely from coal power, that jumps to about 313 grams, slightly worse than our 30-mpg gas car’s 296.

No U.S. state runs on 100% coal, and the grid has gotten cleaner almost every year as coal plants close. The Union of Concerned Scientists found that over 90% of Americans live where driving an average EV produces less global warming pollution than even the most efficient gas car.

The part “zero emissions” leaves out

Carbon isn’t the only kind of environmental damage. EV batteries depend on lithium, nickel, cobalt, and graphite, and getting them out of the ground has real costs:

  • Water. In Chile’s Atacama Desert, one of the driest places on Earth, lithium mining accounts for up to 65% of the region’s water use, according to a United Nations University report, competing with farms and local communities.
  • Human rights. About 70% of the world’s cobalt comes from the Democratic Republic of Congo, where child labor in small-scale mines has been widely documented.
  • Pollution where it’s processed. Nickel mining in Indonesia has been linked to deforestation and water pollution, and refining battery minerals produces far more emissions than digging them up.
  • Recycling is still catching up. Most of today’s EV batteries haven’t reached the end of their lives yet, and recovery of some materials, especially lithium, remains limited.

To be fair, gasoline has its own hidden costs, from oil spills to the pollution near refineries, and burning it fouls the air in the cities where people actually drive. But it’s honest to say that EVs don’t eliminate environmental damage. They shrink the climate damage a lot and move much of the remaining harm to mining regions far from the people who benefit.

The fine print

  • This is a simplified model. Real results depend on the specific car, battery size, where the battery was made, how you drive, and your local grid.
  • Bigger batteries take longer to pay back. A large electric truck or SUV with a huge battery starts further behind than a small EV.
  • The grid keeps changing. Our grid figure is from 2024. A cleaner grid makes every EV mile cleaner over the car’s life.
  • Lifecycle studies differ in their assumptions, which is why we show several.

The bottom line

On climate, the evidence is clear: an EV starts in the hole, climbs out in under two years of driving, and ends up responsible for roughly half to two-thirds less carbon than a gas car. The battery argument is real but much smaller than critics suggest. What “zero emissions” really hides is not carbon but where the damage happens: in the water, mines, and refineries of the places that supply the batteries.
