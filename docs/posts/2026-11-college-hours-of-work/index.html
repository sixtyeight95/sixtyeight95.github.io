College, Measured in Hours of Work

Tuition grew three times faster than pay for 35 years. Then, quietly, it stopped.

If there’s one cost that defines “things were easier for our parents,” it’s college. We wanted to know how much harder it really got, and whether it’s still getting worse. So we measured it the SixtyEight95 way: in hours of work.

The short answer

Since 1980, college tuition has grown 13.8×. Hourly pay grew 4.7×, and prices for everything else grew 4.0×. So a year of college takes about 2.9 times as many hours of work as it did in 1980.

But here’s what almost nobody talks about: college peaked in 2016. Since then, pay has grown faster than tuition, and a year of college now takes about 19% fewer hours of work than at the peak.

How we measure “hours of work” here

For groceries and housing, we could use actual prices. For college, the government doesn’t publish one national “price of college,” because it varies so much: a community college and a private university are worlds apart. What it does publish is a price index, which tracks how much tuition and fees have changed over time for the same kinds of schools.

So we set 1980 = 100 hours and asked: if a year of college took 100 hours of work in 1980, how many hours would the same year of college take in every year since? It’s a measure of change, not a price tag, and it works for any school.

45 years of college, in hours of work

From 1980 to 2016, the line almost only goes up. At the peak, a year of college took 362 hours for every 100 it took in 1980. Today it’s 293.

Tuition vs. pay vs. everything else

Hourly pay and general prices moved almost together for 45 years. College tuition went off on its own.

Researchers point to several reasons for the long climb, including states cutting per-student funding for public colleges (especially after the 2008 recession, which shifted more of the cost onto students), rising demand for degrees, and colleges spending more on staff and facilities.

The twist: college has been getting easier to afford

Since 2016, tuition is up 21% while pay is up 50%. That gap is why the hours-of-work line has been falling for almost a decade.

Two things drove it:

  • Tuition growth slowed down. Many public universities froze or capped tuition, and enrollment has been soft, which limits how much schools can raise prices.
  • Pay sped up. Wages for regular workers grew quickly from 2021 to 2023, faster than they had in years.

College is still far more expensive in work time than it was in 1980. But the story of “it only ever gets worse” isn’t true anymore.

The fine print

  • This is a price index, not a bill. It tracks how tuition and fees changed, not what any one school charges.
  • Grants and scholarships vary. Many families pay less than the posted price, and how much less has changed over time.
  • Tuition isn’t the whole cost. Housing, food, and books are separate, and housing near campus has its own story.
  • Pay is the average worker’s, before taxes. It’s the same pay measure we use in every SixtyEight95 “hours of work” story, so they’re comparable.

The bottom line

College went from expensive to very expensive between 1980 and 2016, rising far faster than paychecks. Since then, the trend has quietly reversed. A year of college still takes nearly three times the work it did in 1980, but less than it did a decade ago.

How we did this. College costs are the Consumer Price Index for “college tuition and fees” (BLS series CUUR0000SEEB01) from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. “Everything else” is the CPI for all items, and pay is average hourly earnings of production and nonsupervisory employees (about 80% of private-sector workers), both via FRED. Hours of work = tuition index ÷ hourly pay, scaled so 1980 = 100. “Today” is the average of November 2025 to October 2026. BLS did not collect October 2025 prices during the federal government shutdown, so that month is skipped.
