What Putting the Holidays on a Credit Card Really Costs

At today’s 22% card rates, $1,000 of gifts paid off at the minimum costs nearly $1,800 and takes about six years. Paying a little more changes everything.

It’s the biggest shopping weekend of the year, and a lot of it goes on plastic. Credit cards are convenient, and if you pay the balance in full you pay no interest at all. But if a holiday balance lingers, today’s interest rates make it expensive. Here’s how expensive.

The short answer

The average interest rate on credit cards that charge interest is 22.4%, up from about 17.0% in 2019. At that rate, $1,000 of holiday gifts paid off with only the minimum payment costs about $1,788 in total and takes about 5.9 years. Paying $100 a month instead clears it in a year for about $117 in interest.

Credit card rates are near record highs

Credit card rates jumped when the Federal Reserve raised interest rates in 2022 and 2023, and they’ve stayed above 20% since. Now the Fed is raising rates again: its benchmark rate went from 3.6% earlier this year to 3.9% in October, with inflation still running high. Card rates usually follow the Fed up.

Americans are carrying about $1.4 trillion in revolving credit, which is mostly credit card debt.

What $1,000 of gifts really costs

We took a $1,000 holiday balance at today’s average rate of 22.4% and asked: how long does it take to pay off, and how much interest do you pay?

Assumes no new charges. Minimum payment = interest + 1% of the balance, or $25, whichever is larger.
How you pay it off Time to pay off Interest paid Total paid
Minimum payment only 5.9 years $788 $1,788
$50 a month 2.2 years $263 $1,263
$100 a month 12 months $117 $1,117
$200 a month 6 months $60 $1,060

The minimum payment starts at about $29 a month. It feels manageable, but most of it goes to interest at first, and the payment shrinks as the balance does. That’s why it takes about 5.9 years.

Bumping the payment to $100 a month cuts the interest by about $671.

How to keep the holidays from following you into 2027

  • Pay the full balance if you can. Most cards charge no interest at all on purchases you pay off by the due date.
  • If you can’t, pick a monthly number and stick to it. Even $50 a month instead of the minimum saves hundreds.
  • Check your card’s actual rate on your statement. Yours may be higher or lower than the average.
  • Be careful with “buy now, pay later.” It can be interest-free, but late fees and stacking several plans add up.

The fine print

  • The card rate is the Federal Reserve’s average for credit card accounts that were charged interest. The average across all accounts, including people who pay in full, is a bit lower.
  • Minimum payment formulas vary by card. We used interest plus 1% of the balance with a $25 floor, a common approach. Check your statement for yours.
  • Our examples assume no new charges and no late fees.
  • Revolving credit includes credit cards and some other revolving lines.

How we did this. Credit card rates are from the Federal Reserve’s G.19 consumer credit release (FRED series TERMCBCCINTNS). Revolving credit is FRED series REVOLSL. The federal funds rate is FEDFUNDS. Payoff math is our own calculation, month by month. Card rates on FRED · Revolving credit on FRED
