How Long Does It Take to Find a Job Now?

Almost nobody is getting laid off. Almost nobody is getting hired. Welcome to the ‘no hire, no fire’ job market.

The unemployment rate looks fine. Layoff headlines are rare. And yet, if you’ve looked for a job recently, you may have felt like you were sending applications into a void. Both things are true. We dug into the government’s job data to explain why.

The short answer

Unemployment is just 4.1%, and layoffs are near record lows. But companies are hiring slowly, so people who do lose a job are stuck looking longer. The average job seeker has been searching for 27.1 weeks, up from about 22 weeks in 2019, and 28% have been out of work for six months or more.

How long job searches last

Two measures: the average (pulled up by people who’ve been looking a very long time) and the median, the typical job seeker. Both have been climbing since 2023. The average is now 27.1 weeks; the median is 11.9 weeks.

(The sharp dip in 2020 isn’t a good sign: tens of millions of people lost their jobs at once in the pandemic, so almost everyone unemployed had just started looking.)

No hire, no fire

Every month, the government counts how many people were hired, laid off, or quit, as a share of all jobs:

Share of all jobs per month. Latest: September 2026 (BLS JOLTS).
Measure Now 2019 average
Hired 3.4% 3.9%
Laid off or fired 1.0% 1.2%
Quit 2.1% 2.3%

Fewer people are being let go than before the pandemic, but fewer are being hired too, and fewer are quitting (people tend to stay put when they’re not confident they’ll land something better). The job market has slowed down without breaking. That’s good news if you have a job, and hard news if you need one.

Why is hiring so slow?

Economists point to a mix of reasons: uncertainty about the economy and interest rates making companies cautious about adding staff, businesses that hired heavily in 2021–2022 still having enough workers, and employers testing whether new technology like AI can handle some tasks before they hire. The data can show that hiring has slowed, but not exactly why, so take any single explanation with a grain of salt.

If you’re job hunting

The numbers suggest planning for a longer search than you might expect:

  • Budget for months, not weeks. The typical search is about 12 weeks, and many take much longer.
  • Don’t quit before you’ve landed something if you can avoid it; quits are down for a reason.
  • Unemployment insurance usually lasts up to 26 weeks in most states, close to the current average search, so it may run out before a long search ends.

The fine print

  • Search length comes from the monthly household survey and counts only people actively looking; people who gave up looking aren’t included.
  • Hiring and layoff rates come from the separate JOLTS employer survey and lag by about a month.
  • National averages hide big differences by industry, location, and experience.

The bottom line

Today’s job market isn’t a recession. Layoffs are near record lows and unemployment is low. But it’s a market that’s stopped moving: if you lose your job, expect the search to take noticeably longer than it would have a few years ago.

All data are from the Bureau of Labor Statistics via FRED: average and median weeks unemployed (UEMPMEAN, UEMPMED), share of unemployed out 27 weeks or more (LNS13025703), the unemployment rate (UNRATE), and JOLTS hires, layoffs and discharges, and quits rates (JTSHIR, JTSLDR, JTSQUR).
