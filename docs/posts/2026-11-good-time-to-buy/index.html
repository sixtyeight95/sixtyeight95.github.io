Home Prices Are Dipping. Is Now a Good Time to Buy?

Prices are down a little. Mortgage rates are up a lot. Here’s what that does to the monthly payment.

You may have seen headlines that home prices are finally coming down. Some are. So is this the moment to buy? We pulled the latest market data to see what’s actually changed for buyers, and what it does to the monthly payment. (This is general information, not financial advice; everyone’s situation is different.)

The short answer

The median listed home is -2.2% cheaper than a year ago. But mortgage rates rose from about 6.25% to 7.33%, so the monthly payment on that typical home is $215 higher: about $2,568 a month, versus $2,353 a year ago.

The real change is leverage: more homes for sale, fewer buyers, and more room to negotiate than in years.

The monthly payment

This is the monthly principal and interest on the median listed home, with 10% down and a 30-year fixed mortgage at that month’s average rate. It doubled from 2021 to 2023 as rates shot up, eased in 2025 as rates fell, and has climbed again this fall.

Are prices really falling?

It depends on which measure you use:

Measure Latest Change from a year ago
Median listing price (Realtor.com) $415,000 -2.2%
Median price of new homes sold (Census) $393,300 -4.1%
Case-Shiller home price index (repeat sales) 336.7 +2.1%

Asking prices and new-home prices are down. But the Case-Shiller index, which tracks the same homes selling again over time and is often the best measure of true price changes, is still slightly up over the year, just off its summer peak. So “prices are dipping” is fair; “prices are falling fast” is not.

The buyer’s market signals

Signal Latest Change from a year ago
Homes listed for sale 1,183,520 +7.4%
Existing homes sold (yearly pace) 3,750,000 -8.8%
Median days on market 63 0.0%
Rents (CPI) +3.0%

More homes for sale and fewer sales means sellers are competing for buyers, not the other way around. That can mean room to negotiate on price, repairs, or seller-paid closing costs. Meanwhile rents are still rising, so waiting isn’t free either.

Things to weigh

The data can’t tell you whether you should buy, but it does point to the questions that matter most:

  • Can you afford the payment at today’s rate? Some people buy hoping to refinance later if rates drop. That can work, but rates aren’t guaranteed to fall, and refinancing has costs.
  • How long will you stay? Buying and selling costs thousands; it usually takes several years for owning to beat renting.
  • Use the leverage. In a slower market, asking for a lower price or concessions is more realistic than it was in 2021–2022.

The fine print

  • Payments shown are principal and interest only. Property taxes, insurance, and HOA fees can add hundreds a month.
  • National medians. Local markets vary widely; some areas are seeing real price drops, others are still rising.
  • The median listing price reflects the mix of homes for sale, not just price changes.

The bottom line

Home prices are softening, and buyers have more choices and leverage than they’ve had in years. But mortgage rates climbed even more than prices dipped, so the typical monthly payment is higher than a year ago. Whether that makes now a good time depends far more on your budget and timeline than on the headlines.

How we did this. Median listing price, active listings, and days on market are from Realtor.com; new-home prices from the Census Bureau; the Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index from S&P; existing home sales from the National Association of Realtors; mortgage rates from Freddie Mac’s weekly survey (averaged by month); and rents from the BLS CPI, all via FRED. Payments assume 10% down and a 30-year fixed loan.
