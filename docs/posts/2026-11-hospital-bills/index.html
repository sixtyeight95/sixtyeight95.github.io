Why Are Hospital Bills So Insanely Expensive?

The same hip replacement costs $29,000 in the U.S. and $7,700 in Germany. Here’s where the money goes.

A night in the hospital can cost more than a used car. A routine birth can come with a bill in the thousands, even with insurance. Everyone knows American hospital care is expensive. We wanted to know how expensive, compared with what, and why.

The short answer

Hospital prices have grown about 17.3× since 1980, while pay grew 4.7×. A hip replacement that private insurers paid $29,006 for in the U.S. cost $7,681 in Germany.

The biggest reasons: private insurers pay hospitals about 254% of what Medicare pays, hospitals have merged into local near-monopolies, about a quarter of hospital spending goes to administration, and hospital “list prices” are inflated far beyond cost.

45 years of hospital prices, in hours of work

Like our other cost-of-living stories, we measured hospital prices against what the average worker earns. If a hospital service took 100 hours of work to pay for in 1980, here’s how many hours the same service has taken since.

Hospital care now takes about 3.7 times as many hours of work as it did in 1980. Unlike college tuition, which has started getting easier to afford, hospital prices have stayed near their peak.

The same procedure, around the world

The International Federation of Health Plans compared what private insurers paid for the same hospital procedures in nine countries in 2022. These are actual payments, not list prices.

Why: the four big reasons

1. Private insurance pays hospitals more than double what Medicare pays

The same hospital, the same procedure, very different prices depending on who’s paying. RAND studied claims from employer health plans across the country and found that in 2022, they paid hospitals 254% of what Medicare would have paid for the same care. In some states it was over 300%.

Medicare sets its prices. Private insurers have to negotiate them, and often they don’t have much leverage, which leads to reason two.

2. Hospitals have merged into local near-monopolies

Over the past few decades, hospitals have bought each other up. KFF found that in 47% of U.S. metro areas, the entire hospital market is controlled by just one or two health systems. When an insurer has to include a hospital system in its network because there’s no alternative, that system can charge more. Research consistently finds that hospital mergers raise prices, without clear improvements in quality.

3. A quarter of the budget goes to paperwork

Billing in the U.S. is complicated: hundreds of insurers, each with its own rules, codes, and approval processes. One study found that administration took about 25% of U.S. hospital budgets, about twice the share in Canada or Scotland. (The study’s authors support a single-payer system, but the gap they measured is large under any interpretation.)

4. The “list price” is mostly fiction

Every hospital has a “chargemaster,” a master list of prices. These list prices bear little resemblance to what care costs. A study of Medicare cost data found the average hospital listed $4.32 for every $1 of its actual costs, and up to about 28 times cost for CT scans.

Medicare and insurers rarely pay list prices. The people who often get billed them are the uninsured and patients treated out of network, the ones with the least power to negotiate.

The human cost

About 20 million adults owe significant medical debt (over $250), totaling at least $220 billion, according to KFF. Hospital bills are a big part of that.

The fine print

  • Different years. Country prices and the Medicare comparison are from 2022, the administrative cost study is from 2011 data, and the list price study from 2013. The patterns have held up in later research, but the exact numbers have moved.
  • Country prices are medians of what private insurers paid, converted to U.S. dollars by the report’s authors. Public systems in some countries may pay less.
  • “Hours of work” uses the government’s hospital services price index and the average worker’s hourly pay before taxes. It tracks how prices changed, not any one bill.
  • What you personally pay depends on your insurance, deductible, and whether the hospital is in network.

The bottom line

Health economists have been saying it for decades: American health care is expensive mainly because the prices are higher, not because Americans get far more care. For hospitals, private insurers pay far more than Medicare, merged hospital systems have the leverage to keep it that way, billing complexity adds a heavy layer of cost, and inflated list prices land hardest on the people least able to pay.
