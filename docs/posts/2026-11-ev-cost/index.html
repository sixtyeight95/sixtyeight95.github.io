Does an Electric Car Actually Save You Money?

EVs are much cheaper to run. They’re not cheaper to own, at least not new. Here’s the math, per year.

Electric car fans say you’ll save a fortune on gas. Skeptics say EVs cost more and lose value fast. They’re both partly right. To settle it, we compared the full cost of owning an EV, a gas car, and a hybrid of the same size and class, per year.

The short answer

An EV saves you about $1,728 a year on fuel and maintenance (for a medium sedan). But it loses value so much faster that a new EV costs $3,080 more per year to own than a comparable gas sedan. In SUVs, it’s roughly a tie. And in every class, a hybrid was cheapest.

How we compared them

We used AAA’s 2026 Your Driving Costs study, which averages the top-selling 2026 models in each class and adds up everything it costs to own one for five years at 15,000 miles a year:

  • Fuel or charging
  • Maintenance and repairs, including tires
  • Depreciation: how much value the car loses
  • Insurance (full coverage)
  • License, registration, and taxes, including the extra EV fees most states now charge
  • Finance charges: interest on the car loan

That puts every vehicle on the same footing: dollars per year.

Where EVs win: running costs

The EV advantage is real. For a medium sedan, charging cost about $712 a year versus $2,215 for gas, and maintenance was cheaper too (no oil changes, fewer moving parts, and brakes that last longer thanks to regenerative braking).

One big catch: where you charge. AAA’s EV fuel costs line up with home electricity prices, which average about $0.19 per kWh nationally. Public fast chargers typically cost around $0.45 per kWh, about 2.3 times more. If you can’t charge at home and rely on fast chargers, a medium sedan’s charging bill rises to roughly $1,661 a year: still less than gas, but most of the savings are gone.

Where EVs lose: owning costs

The biggest single cost of owning any new car is depreciation, the value it loses while you own it. For EVs, it’s brutal. AAA puts a new electric medium sedan’s depreciation at $6,786 a year, versus $3,330 for gas. Fast-improving battery technology and price cuts on new EVs make used ones worth less.

EVs also tend to cost more to insure (they’re pricier to repair), most states now charge an extra annual EV registration fee (often $100 to $250) to make up for lost gas-tax revenue, and a pricier car means more loan interest. And as of September 30, 2025, the $7,500 federal EV tax credit is gone.

So who should buy an EV?

The numbers point to a few clear cases where an EV makes financial sense:

  • You can charge at home. That’s where the fuel savings come from.
  • You drive a lot. More miles means more fuel savings to offset the extra ownership costs.
  • You keep cars a long time. Depreciation is heaviest in the first few years. Keep the car 10 years and the per-year cost of that early drop shrinks.
  • You buy used. The first owner already took the big depreciation hit. A used EV can capture the running-cost savings without paying for the steepest part of the value drop.
  • You’re shopping SUVs, not sedans. The gap is smallest there.

If saving money is the top priority and none of those fit, the data say: buy a hybrid. It beat both EVs and gas cars in every class AAA measured.

The fine print

  • These are averages across top-selling models. A specific EV with a big discount, or a gas guzzler, can change the answer.
  • Five-year ownership is AAA’s assumption. Longer ownership generally helps EVs.
  • Electricity and gas prices vary a lot by state. Cheap power and expensive gas favor EVs.
  • Public charging prices vary by network and membership; ours is a typical 2026 figure.
  • This post is about money. Whether EVs are better for the environment is a separate question, and our follow-up post tackles it.

The bottom line

EVs really are cheaper to run. But “cheaper to run” isn’t “cheaper to own.” Buying new, without the old federal tax credit, an EV costs about the same as a gas SUV and more than a gas sedan, because it loses value so fast. If you can charge at home, drive a lot, keep cars for years, or buy used, the math tilts toward electric. Otherwise, a hybrid is the money winner.
